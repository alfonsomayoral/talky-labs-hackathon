# Accounting Course: Eight Modules

Study notes based on the lessons at [conta-course-creator.lovable.app](https://conta-course-creator.lovable.app/). They summarize useful content, examples, and controls. Tax rates and references in the course are for educational purposes: verify the applicable rules and effective date before using them outside the exercise.

## Module 1 — Accounting mechanics and universal principles

### Double-entry bookkeeping and account signs

Every transaction affects at least two elements, and total debits must equal total credits in each journal entry. Assets and expenses increase with debits; liabilities, equity, and revenue increase with credits. Decreases are recorded on the opposite side. “Debit” names the left-hand column; it does not mean a negative valuation.

### Accrual basis and cash basis

Recognize an expense when a resource is consumed or a service is received; payment records the cash outflow. Recognize revenue when a service is performed or goods are delivered; collection records the cash inflow. Different timing creates balance-sheet balances such as receivables, payables, advances, and prepayments/accruals.

Examples from the lesson:

- Credit purchase of supplies: Debit expense / Credit accounts payable. The expense arises even if payment comes later.
- A €3,000 service with €1,000 collected and €2,000 outstanding: Debit bank €1,000 and receivables €2,000 / Credit revenue €3,000.
- Rent paid in advance for the next quarter is initially an asset representing the right to receive the service, not an expense for the full amount on payment.

### Principles and accounting cycle

The course highlights going concern, accrual, consistency, prudence, no offsetting, and materiality. The accounting cycle runs opening balances → journal → general ledger → trial balance → adjustments → income statement closing → books closing → annual financial statements. A balanced trial balance catches arithmetic imbalances, but not an incorrectly selected account.

Accounting works as an auditable event log: do not erase an incorrect entry; correct it with a reversing or correcting entry. To build a new entry, ask what changed, which account category it belongs to, whether it increased or decreased, and which side to use.

## Module 2 — Balance sheet

### Structure

`Assets = Liabilities + Equity`; therefore `Equity = Assets − Liabilities`. Assets describe how resources are used; liabilities and equity describe how they are financed. Equity includes contributed capital, reserves, and profit or loss, and is non-repayable financing.

- **Non-current assets:** resources used for more than one year, such as machinery, vehicles, software, or long-term investments.
- **Current assets:** inventory, trade receivables, short-term investments, and cash that turn over in the operating cycle.
- **Non-current liabilities:** obligations due in more than twelve months.
- **Current liabilities:** obligations due within twelve months, including suppliers, taxes, and the portion of long-term debt due in the next twelve months.

Assets are commonly ordered from least to most liquid; liabilities from least to most urgent. Classify by function and term: a building used by the company may be property, plant, and equipment; one acquired for resale is inventory.

### Balance sheet, income statement, and liquidity

The balance sheet is a position at a date; the income statement reports revenue and expenses over a period; the cash flow statement explains cash receipts and payments. Period profit appears in equity. Profit is not the same as cash.

The lesson introduces these indicators:

- Working capital = current assets − current liabilities.
- Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities.
- Leverage = total liabilities / total financing (liabilities + equity).
- Financial autonomy = equity / total assets.
- Asset turnover = revenue / total assets.

These are prompts for investigation, not standalone conclusions. The module gives illustrative autonomy and liquidity values; interpret them in light of sector, seasonality, and asset quality. A high receivables balance may signal slow collection; high inventory, low turnover; high cash, idle funds.

## Module 3 — Suppliers and accounts payable

### Pre-payment controls

A *three-way match* compares the purchase order (what and at what price was authorized), receiving report (what was received in acceptable condition), and invoice (what is being charged). Check quantities, prices, specifications, due date, discounts, and taxes. Block and investigate an unauthorized discrepancy; do not pay first and dispute it later.

The four control questions are: Was it ordered? Was it received? Is the amount agreed? Is it authorized and still outstanding? Separation of duties among requester, receiver, and payer reduces fraud risk.

### Recognition and accounting treatment

A purchase order alone is a commitment, not necessarily a liability. Receipt of goods or services creates the accrual; payment settles the payable. If goods were received by period-end and the invoice arrives later, an accrued uninvoiced payable may need to be recognized. An advance paid to a supplier is an asset until applied to a purchase.

- Trade discount shown on the invoice: reduces purchase cost.
- Subsequent volume rebate: reduces cost under the applicable accounting treatment.
- Early-payment discount: the course treats it as financial income.
- Return: record a credit in the opposite direction; do not delete the original entry.
- Deductible input VAT: a recoverable tax amount, separate from expense; non-deductible VAT forms part of cost.
- Accounts 400 for trade suppliers and 410 for other service creditors, following the course example and subject to verification against the applicable chart of accounts.

The course presents average days payable as an indicator and recommends monitoring due dates; any cited legal limit must be checked for country, industry, and effective date.

## Module 4 — Customers and accounts receivable

### Revenue, collection, and advances

Revenue arises when goods are delivered or services performed (and can be measured reliably), not when a contract is signed or cash is collected. A customer advance is a liability until the performance obligation is fulfilled. Collecting an already-recognized invoice changes receivables to cash; it does not create new profit.

### Aging and impairment

An aging report groups balances by due date to manage reminders, collection actions, credit holds, and risk. The course proposes aging buckets as an operational guide; they are not universal impairment percentages.

An impairment allowance reduces the net carrying value of a receivable and recognizes a loss when there is evidence of credit risk. It does not automatically forgive or cancel the debt. Distinguish:

1. Reclassify the balance as doubtful (for example, 430 → 436 in the course's account numbering).
2. Record the impairment allowance (accounts 694/490 in the example).
3. Reverse the allowance if the risk disappears, or write off the debt only when it is extinguished or uncollectible under the applicable policy.

An individual assessment relies on specific indicators such as insolvency proceedings or litigation; a statistical portfolio method requires a homogeneous portfolio and reliable history. DSO is presented as `(average receivables balance / sales including VAT for the period) × days in the period`; define sales and balances consistently when comparing periods.

The course lists bill discounting, factoring with or without recourse, confirming, and credit insurance. Each option allocates liquidity, cost, and risk differently; do not assume that assigning a receivable eliminates risk without checking the contract.

## Module 5 — Accounting books and bank reconciliation

### Journal and general ledger

The journal records entries chronologically; the general ledger groups the journal lines by account. The trial balance checks totals and balances. Ideal traceability is supporting document → journal entry → general ledger → trial balance/financial statements. A negative cash balance or unsupported figure requires investigation.

### Reconcile the bank

Compare the official bank statement and account 572 at the same date. Match lines, separate items appearing only at the bank from those appearing only in the books, and classify each difference:

- **Timing:** an issued check not yet cashed or a deposit in transit; no entry is needed.
- **Omission:** an unrecorded fee, interest amount, or receipt; record an entry.
- **Error:** wrong amount, account, or duplicate; correct it with supporting evidence.
- **Foreign currency:** distinguish exchange differences (examples 668/768) from bank fees (626).

After recording omissions and correcting errors, timing items should explain the remaining balance differences. Look for combinations of invoices that make up grouped payments; reconcile each invoice separately in its subledger. Preserve open balances for partial receipts or payments. A returned payment may reopen the customer's balance and also incur a fee.

### Foreign currency and cash management

The lesson illustrates recording transactions at the exchange rate on the transaction date, remeasuring outstanding monetary items at period-end, and recognizing realized or valuation differences in financial income or expense. Non-monetary assets carried at historical cost are not remeasured in the same way. Confirm the treatment and accounts in the applicable policy.

Monthly reconciliation, cash counts, and short-term cash receipt/payment forecasts are recommended. No journal entry should lack supporting documentation.

## Module 6 — Fixed assets and depreciation

### Recognition and cost

Capitalize controlled resources that will be used for more than one period when their cost can be measured; expense resources consumed in the current period. Initial cost includes what is necessary to bring the asset to operating condition, including non-deductible VAT. Training and routine maintenance are generally not capitalized; an improvement that increases capacity or useful life may qualify under policy. Depreciation begins when the asset is available for use.

### Depreciation

Straight-line charge = `(acquisition cost − residual value) / useful life`. Prorate the first period from the date the asset becomes available for use. The expense account (class 68 in the example) is debited against accumulated depreciation (class 28); do not directly reduce gross cost. Net book value = cost − accumulated depreciation.

Methods mentioned include straight-line (constant), declining balance (higher expense initially), and units of production (based on usage). Land with an indefinite useful life is not depreciated. Impairment, depreciation, and maintenance are distinct concepts.

When selling or retiring an asset, remove its cost and accumulated depreciation and compare proceeds with net book value to calculate the gain or loss. The course distinguishes book depreciation from tax depreciation: differences are adjusted outside the accounts under current tax rules.

## Module 7 — VAT, withholding, and corporate income tax

### VAT

Output VAT on sales is a liability to the tax authority; deductible input VAT is a recoverable tax amount. They are generally not revenue and expense, respectively. The course presents the VAT settlement as `output VAT − deductible input VAT`: a positive amount is payable; a negative amount is carried forward or refunded according to the applicable regime.

The right to deduct depends on a valid invoice, business use, recording, timing, and statutory exclusions. VAT on exempt transactions may not be deductible and may become part of cost. Actual treatment depends on rate, exemption, place of supply, and transaction regime.

### Withholding

Withholding is deducted from payments to a contractor, landlord, or employee and remitted to the tax authority on their behalf. It is a temporary liability, not a reduction in expense. For example, a contractor invoice has a €1,000 base, €210 VAT, and €150 withholding; €1,060 is paid and €150 is payable to the tax authority. For payroll, employer cost includes gross pay and employer contributions; net pay and withholdings/contributions explain the other sides of the entry.

### Corporate income tax

The taxable base starts with accounting profit and is adjusted under tax rules; non-deductible fines are added back, and temporary differences may arise from depreciation. Loss carryforwards are then applied, followed by the tax rate, credits, and instalment payments to calculate the balance payable or refundable. Do not equate accounting profit, taxable income, and the bank balance.

Rates, deduction requirements, forms, deadlines, and tax accounts in this lesson must not be applied to a real transaction without checking current rules and jurisdiction.

## Module 8 — Period-end close and adjustments

### Accruals and prepayments: four cases

| Event | Nature at period-end | Effect on profit or loss |
|---|---|---|
| Paid, not yet consumed (future insurance) | Prepaid asset | Reduces current-year expense |
| Collected, service not yet provided (future subscription) | Deferred revenue liability | Reduces current-year revenue |
| Consumed, not paid or invoiced (utility) | Accrued liability | Increases current-year expense |
| Provided, not collected or invoiced | Accrued asset | Increases current-year revenue |

Assigning expenses and revenue to the correct period separates the economic date from the cash receipt, payment, or invoicing date.

### Inventory and valuation adjustments

Consumption = opening inventory + net purchases − closing inventory. A physical count identifies shrinkage, breakage, and obsolescence. Weighted average cost averages unit costs; FIFO assumes the oldest units are consumed first and leaves ending inventory at the cost of more recent purchases. Closing valuation affects margin and profit.

The close also reviews reclassification of debt/receivables due within the next twelve months, corrections through adjusting entries, depreciation, impairment, and taxes.

### Sequence and result

The lesson orders the close as a pipeline: record outstanding transactions → reconcile banks and counterparties → adjust inventory → depreciate and impair → accrue and defer → settle taxes → close income and expense accounts → prepare annual financial statements. Revenue and expense classes are closed to current-year profit or loss (account 129 in the example); assets, liabilities, and equity carry forward as opening balances for the next period.

Profit or loss links the income statement to equity. The annual financial statements described include the balance sheet, income statement, notes, statement of changes in equity, and cash flow statement, with formats and requirements depending on company size and applicable rules.

## Cross-cutting lessons

1. Record the economic event with appropriate evidence and in the correct period.
2. Distinguish economic flows (revenue/expense) from cash flows (collection/payment).
3. Use a subledger by customer, supplier, and document; a balanced control account alone is not enough.
4. A reconciliation explains differences; it does not hide them with a generic entry.
5. Classify and measure closing assets and liabilities using evidence and consistent policy.
6. Equal debits and credits are necessary, but do not prove the transaction was classified correctly.
